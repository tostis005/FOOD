# FOOD editorial standard

This file defines non-negotiable editorial rules for generated and revised articles.

## 1. Comparisons must be quantified

Whenever an article compares foods, portions, nutrients, calories, storage times, temperatures or other measurable properties, give the numbers for both sides whenever a reliable like-for-like value exists.

Bad:

> An apple or orange has much less energy than avocado.

Better:

> Avocado provides about 160 kcal per 100 g, compared with roughly 52 kcal for apple and 47 kcal for orange per 100 g.

Bad:

> Cooked lentils contain less protein per 100 g than dry lentils.

Better:

> Dry lentils contain roughly 24–25 g protein per 100 g, while cooked lentils contain about 9 g per 100 g because they absorb water during cooking.

The purpose is not keyword density. It is to let the reader understand the magnitude of the difference.

### Apply this rule to

- more / less protein, fiber, fat, carbohydrate, sodium, sugar, calories or micronutrients;
- more / less caloric, lean, concentrated, dense or satiating when the statement refers to a measurable food property;
- raw vs cooked, dry vs cooked, fresh vs canned, drained vs undrained;
- one food vs another food;
- one serving size vs another;
- storage-time or temperature comparisons;
- rankings and statements such as “much higher”, “roughly twice”, “half as much” or “similar”.

### Exceptions

A number is not required when the comparison is genuinely qualitative and a numerical value would be artificial or misleading, for example “a colder refrigerator slows microbial growth” or “a thinner container cools food faster”. Do not invent precision.

## 2. Compare like with like, but write for people

State preparation or edible state only when it materially changes the answer: raw, cooked, grilled/pan-cooked, canned, drained, dry, with/without skin, etc. Prefer ordinary culinary language over database terminology. Do not write labels such as “cooked with dry heat” when “cooked” or “grilled/pan-cooked” communicates the point clearly enough.

Avoid rankings that mix radically different states without explanation. If preparation changes the values enough to matter, either use one consistent state for the main ranking or separate the states into clearly labelled comparisons. Do not make the reader mentally compare a fresh fillet, an oil-packed canned product and a dried product as if they were the same thing.

Do not rank overlapping ranges. Use concrete reference values when order matters, but present those values in a reader-friendly way.

## 3. Explain why the numbers differ

Do not stop at the table. Explain the mechanism when useful: water gain/loss, fat content, concentration during drying, salt curing, edible bone, serving size, etc.

## 4. Every article must stand on its own

The reader should be able to finish an article and understand the question it promised to answer without being told that an important point “deserves another article”. Related articles can deepen a topic, but the current article must contain enough context to make its own claims intelligible.

Avoid phrases such as:

- “this deserves a separate article”;
- “we will cover this elsewhere”;
- “this is better discussed in another article”;
- “we deliberately exclude…”;
- “we deliberately round…”;
- “this table is not intended to…”;
- “we do not pretend that…”;
- “for methodological reasons we chose…” unless that methodological detail is essential to understand the result.

Replace editorial commentary with useful reader-facing information. For example, instead of saying that decimal differences were deliberately rounded because they are not useful, say what the reader actually needs to know: “The difference between 23.8 and 23.5 g of protein per 100 g is small enough that portion size, species and cooking water loss can matter more in practice.”

## 5. No visible author/process commentary

Articles should not sound as if the writer is explaining how the article was assembled. Methodology belongs only where it changes interpretation. Avoid defensive or self-referential phrases such as “we used”, “we chose”, “we excluded”, “we rounded”, “our ranking”, “the register used”, “in this article we prefer”, or similar wording when the same information can be stated directly.

Bad:

> Redondearlas es deliberado: una diferencia de unas décimas no tiene utilidad práctica cuando el contenido real también cambia con la especie concreta, el tamaño de la pieza y la pérdida de agua durante la cocción.

Better:

> Una diferencia de unas décimas por 100 g es pequeña. En la práctica, la especie concreta, el tamaño de la pieza y la pérdida de agua durante la cocción pueden modificar más el valor final.

Bad:

> Las recomendaciones para embarazadas y niños merecen un artículo específico.

Better:

> El atún aporta mucha proteína, pero las especies grandes también pueden acumular más mercurio. Por eso, además de la proteína, conviene tener en cuenta las recomendaciones de consumo para grupos sensibles.

## 6. Sources belong in the article when they matter

Use primary or high-authority sources whenever practical (AESAN/EFSA/BEDCA for Spanish/EU context; USDA/FDA/CDC and equivalent authorities for U.S./English context). Cite or explain the source when it materially affects interpretation, but do not turn the prose into a description of the research process.

## 7. Spanish and English are localized articles, not literal translations

They share a `translation_group`, but examples, authorities, food-safety guidance and wording can differ by market. English articles should normally use U.S./general-English references where that better matches user intent; Spanish articles should normally use Spain/EU context.

## 8. No template prose

Use the structure that best answers the query. Tables, FAQs and recurring components are tools, not mandatory slots. Avoid a repetitive “H2 + two sentences” rhythm. Each article should have enough explanatory depth to stand on its own as a reference-quality answer.

## 9. Prefer clarity over database precision

Nutrition databases are sources, not the voice of the article. Translate technical preparation labels into normal culinary language where this does not change meaning. Keep exact decimals when they matter to an ordered ranking or calculation, but do not make the prose feel like a database export.

A reader should understand at a glance whether a number refers to raw food, normally cooked food, canned/drained food or a dry product. If those categories would make a table confusing, simplify the comparison rather than adding more technical labels.
